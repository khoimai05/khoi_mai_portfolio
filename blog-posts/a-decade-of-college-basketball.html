A Decade of College Basketball December 2024
Mackey Arena is the loudest and most magical sports environment I've ever been to. It's the pride of Purdue Basketball and an impenetrable fortress (most of the time). This is where I fell in love with college basketball. The passion, the unpredictableness, the rivalries, and at the center of it all, the love for the game of basketball.
As a self-proclaimed ball knower, I figured it would be fun to look through the data of the last 10 years to see what has changed and build a model to predict the Final 4 of the last four March Madness.
I leveraged the pandasql library to execute SQL queries on the dataset, incorporating CTEs (Common Table Expressions) and table joins to streamline complex data manipulations. For visualization, I utilized Matplotlib and Seaborn to highlight patterns and trends within the data.
The Rise of the Three-Pointer
First, let's talk about one of the most monumental changes in college basketball this past decade, the use of three-pointers.
Before 2014, approximately 1 out of every three field goals attempted was behind the three-point line. After, the 3-point attempted rate has increased consistently before peaking at 38.7% in 2019. It then dipped back to 37.5% in 2020 (when the three-point line was moved back) before consistently hovering above 37% since.
Interestingly, although teams have attempted more three-pointers per game this past decade, the three-point made percentage has remained practically unchanged.
Although peaking at an efficient 34.97% in 2018, 3PM% has decreased dramatically and bottomed at 33.2% in 2020 and going back up to 33.7% in the last season.
The Final Four Landscape
Since 2013, an astounding 30 different teams have been to the Final Four. 11 teams have been there more than once and North Carolina, Villanova, and Connecticut topped the table with three appearances. Out of these three, Uconn has won every time they've been to the Final Four. This is what I love about college basketball: everyone has a chance to write their own story. Whether it's about a college basketball powerhouse dominating everyone to win a well-deserving championship or an underdog against all odds defeating the Goliath, something special is always in the making.
Although there are a total of 32 conferences in D1, 6 stood out as basketball power conferences. These are: Big Ten, Big Twelve, SEC, ACC, PAC 12, and Big East. They all made up a huge portion of teams in March.
Predicting the Final 4 is not as easy as picking the top seed of each region. The average seed of the Final Four teams throughout the years is never below 2, which indicates that there hasn't been a Final Four of all 1 and 2 seeds in the last decade.
Modeling
I have dropped all the teams that weren't selected for March Madness and encoded the Postseason accomplishments of all teams from a scale of 0 to 7 (R68 to Champion).
df.dropna(subset=['POSTSEASON'], inplace=True)
round_mapping = {
    'R68': 0, 'R64': 1, 'R32': 2, 'S16': 3,
    'E8': 4, 'F4': 5, '2ND': 6, 'Champions': 7
}
df['POSTSEASON'] = df['POSTSEASON'].map(round_mapping)
I then apply Min-Max Scaling since this would make all the data fall between 0 and 1. Min-Max Scaling helps standardize the data range without distorting the relationships among the variables, ensuring that no single feature dominates the model due to its magnitude. One of the drawbacks is that it's sensitive to outliers but since we are trying to find the top 4 teams of every year, I believe this would work perfectly. Furthermore, we are scaling by year since the level of competition each year is different since a final-four caliber team this year could be a sweet-sixteen team a different year.
columns = [
    "ADJEM", "ADJOE", "ADJDE", "EFG_O", "EFG_D", "TOM", "TOR",
    "RBM", "TORD", "ORB", "DRB", "FTR", "FTRD", "2P_O", "2P_D",
    "3P_O", "3P_D", "ADJ_T", "WAB", "Year", "BARTHAG"
]

from sklearn.preprocessing import MinMaxScaler
scaler = MinMaxScaler()
scaled_df = pd.DataFrame()

for year, group in df.groupby("Year"):
    scaled_data = scaler.fit_transform(group[columns])
    scaled_group = pd.DataFrame(scaled_data, columns=columns, index=group.index)
    scaled_group["Year"] = year
    scaled_df = pd.concat([scaled_df, scaled_group])

df[columns] = scaled_df[columns]
I tried to engineer some new features and have come up with three:
1. ADJEM (Adjusted Efficiency Margin): Adjusted Offensive Efficiency − Adjusted Defensive Efficiency
2. Power Conferences: whether or not they are in one
3. RBM (Rebound Margin): Offensive Rebound Rate − Offensive Rebound Allowed
4. TOM (Turnover Margin): Steal rate − Turnover rate
Approach
I began with a baseline model that predicts the Final Four teams by selecting the team with the highest ADJEM (Adjusted Efficiency Margin) from each region. This straightforward approach provides a benchmark for comparison with more sophisticated models.
To enhance prediction accuracy, I then implemented models using Logistic Regression and a Random Forest Classifier, hoping to leverage their strengths in classification tasks to capture more nuanced relationships in the data.
